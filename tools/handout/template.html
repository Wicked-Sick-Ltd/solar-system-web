Every record is drawn from NASA/JPL and the IAU Minor Planet Center, rebuilt nightly, and free to browse, query or download. Built and maintained by Wicked Sick Ltd.

What you can do with it

Browse & search

Search 1.5 million objects by name, designation or discoverer. Filter and sort by type, size, orbit class or discovery date — every view has a shareable URL.

See it move

An interactive orrery plots the planets where they actually are. Object pages carry orbital elements, physical data, rings, moons and close approaches.

Teach and explore

A daily spotlight and a random-object button make it easy to start anywhere. Ideal for classrooms, hobby astronomers, writers and worldbuilders.

One thing to be clear about

This is astronomy, not astrology.

No horoscopes, houses, transits or natal charts — just observational data from NASA/JPL and the IAU, presented plainly and kept current.

Where the planets are — {{DATE_HUMAN}}

Eight worlds, right now

Each dial looks down on the Solar System from above the Sun’s north pole. The dotted line is the planet’s own orbit drawn to its true shape; the small grey dot marks perihelion, its closest point to the Sun. Angles are heliocentric ecliptic longitude, measured anticlockwise from the March equinox direction (0°). Dials are not to a common scale — each orbit is drawn to fill its own dial. This sheet is a dated snapshot, correct for the date above — re-run tools/handout/generate.py at any time for a current one.

Then and now — moons known in 1991 vs today

Four hundred and twenty new moons

When Deuteros shipped on the Amiga in 1991, the whole solar system came to sixty known moons. You could have listed every one of them on a single sheet of paper. The counts below are live from the catalogue; the 1991 column is the historical record.

In 1991 Pluto was still a planet and Charon was its only known companion; it now has five.

How this was counted

Today’s figures are the catalogue’s own, read live from the API. The 1991 column is the historical record as it stood at the end of that year. Two moons sit in the “found since” column despite older discovery dates: Jupiter’s Themisto was seen in 1975, lost, and not recovered until 2000; Uranus’s Perdita was imaged by Voyager 2 in 1986 but went unnoticed until 1999 and was not confirmed until 2003. Neither was a known moon in 1991.

Moon counts move. New outer satellites are announced regularly, so treat this page as a dated snapshot like the last one.
